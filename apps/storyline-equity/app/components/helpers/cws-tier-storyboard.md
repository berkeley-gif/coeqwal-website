# Community water systems to tiers: revised storyboard

## Narrative role

This sequence begins with the metro map already established by the preceding
story. Rivers and canals form the lines; major water-user groups are the
stations. We do not introduce a new visual metaphor. We reveal that each
station is also a node where COEQWAL can examine an outcome.

The worked example follows a single community water system, `25_PU`:

`station → 100 simulated water years → annual delivery versus annual demand →`
`percent demand met → distribution → discrete tier → continuous tendency →`
`all community-water-system nodes`

Section 08 preserves the existing argument about why COEQWAL is needed.
Section 09 demonstrates how the shared tier framework works.
Section 10 returns to the distributional-equity purpose.

Technical tier thresholds remain outside the storyboard.

---

## 08 Resolution — from different measures to a shared framework

### Frame 08.1 — Return to the system

**On-screen text**

> Water decisions affect communities and ecosystems in different ways.

**Visualization**

- Begin on the completed metro map.
- Keep the major user-group stations introduced earlier: agriculture,
  communities, wetlands, and fish/ecosystems.
- Lines and stations are fully resolved; nothing new enters yet.

**Narrative purpose**

This reconnects the equity story to the physical water system and retains the
opening idea from the existing Section 08: COEQWAL cannot undo historical
inequities, but it can make their present-day impacts visible.

### Frame 08.2 — Different outcomes, different measures

**On-screen text**

> Those impacts are measured in very different ways—from water deliveries and
> farm production to river flows, salinity, and fish populations.

**Visualization**

- Give each major station type one small, distinct measurement glyph.
- Keep the glyphs restrained: volume, revenue, flow, salinity, and abundance.
- Do not introduce detailed charts; the mismatch between measures is the point.

**Motion**

- Measurement glyphs appear beside their corresponding station types.
- Their different shapes and units make comparison feel visually difficult.

### Frame 08.3 — Why a common view is needed

**On-screen text**

> Because these outcomes use different units and scales, it can be hard to
> compare them—or to see who benefits and who bears the strain.

**Visualization**

- The measurement glyphs briefly align but fail to share a common axis.
- Stations remain fixed in the metro network.
- Dim the glyphs and return attention to the stations themselves.

**Narrative purpose**

This condenses the core argument currently split across Sections 08 and 09:
different measures make trade-offs and distributions difficult to read.

### Frame 08.4 — COEQWAL’s response

**On-screen text**

> COEQWAL brings these outcomes into a shared framework, making water
> trade-offs visible, comparable, and accessible.

**Visualization**

- Each station simplifies to a common circular node while retaining a subtle
  user-group identifier.
- Community-water-system nodes become the active group.
- Other user groups recede but remain visible as context.

**Motion**

- Hold the network in place.
- Emphasize one community-water-system node: `25_PU`.

### Frame 08.5 — Begin with one community

**On-screen text**

> To see how the framework works, begin with one community water system.

**Visualization**

- Label the selected station `25_PU`.
- Fade the rest of the network to a quiet background.
- Keep the selected node in its geographic/network position at first.

**Transition to Section 09**

- The network moves outward or softens.
- `25_PU` moves to the center without changing shape.
- The node remains the stable object through every following transformation.

## 09 Tiers — from simulated deliveries to a common yardstick

### Frame 09.1 — Simulate variability

**On-screen text**

> For each scenario, COEQWAL simulates water deliveries across 100 years of
> hydrologic variability.

> These years are not a forecast or timeline. Together, they represent a wide
> range of wetter and drier conditions.

**Visualization**

- Place `25_PU` at the left or center as the source node.
- Reveal 100 small water-year marks as an unordered field, not a chronological
  line.
- Use a 10 × 10 field or gently packed distribution.
- Label the field `100 simulated water years`.
- Do not display calendar years or imply past-to-future movement.

**Motion**

- Marks radiate from `25_PU` and settle simultaneously or in short groups.
- Avoid left-to-right sequential animation, which would imply chronology.

### Frame 09.2 — Delivery and demand in each water year

**On-screen text**

> At `25_PU`, we compare annual surface-water delivery with annual drinking-water
> demand in each water year.

**Visualization**

- Expand several representative water-year marks into paired vertical bars:
  `delivery` and `demand`.
- Both bars use the same TAF scale.
- Then repeat the paired-bar structure across all 100 water years as compact
  small multiples.
- Maintain October-to-September water-year aggregation in the data, but the
  monthly calculation does not need to appear on screen.

**Motion**

- One paired comparison establishes the reading.
- It then repeats across the entire 100-year field.

### Frame 09.3 — Convert each comparison to a percentage

**On-screen text**

> Each comparison becomes the percentage of drinking-water demand met.

**Visualization**

- Each delivery/demand pair collapses into one circular percentage mark.
- Encode percent demand met by vertical position on a 0–100% scale.
- Cap the display at 100%, matching the outcome metric.
- Directly label the axis `Demand met`.
- Show only a few value labels to demonstrate the conversion.

**Motion**

- Paired bars converge into single dots.
- All 100 dots retain a one-to-one identity with their simulated water years.

### Frame 09.4 — Reveal the distribution

**On-screen text**

> Viewed together, the 100 years show how reliably deliveries meet demand—and
> how severe shortfalls can become.

**Visualization**

- Rearrange the 100 percentage marks into a distribution for `25_PU`.
- Preferred form: a beeswarm or stacked dot distribution on a horizontal
  `0–100% demand met` axis.
- A cluster near 100% communicates reliability; the lower tail communicates
  shortfall severity.
- Do not connect the dots with a line.
- Do not label technical thresholds.

**Motion**

- Dots sort by percentage rather than by year.
- This is the moment that explicitly removes any remaining temporal reading.

### Frame 09.5 — Translate the pattern into a tier

**On-screen text**

> COEQWAL translates this pattern into a tier—a common yardstick for reading
> conditions across very different outcomes.

**Visualization**

- The distribution compresses back into the `25_PU` node.
- Reveal the four named conditions:
  `Optimal · Acceptable · At-risk · Critical`.
- Place `25_PU` in `At-risk`.
- The four tiers should read as an interpretive scale, not four formulas.

**Motion**

- The 100 dots gather into the single node.
- The node lands in the At-risk region.

### Frame 09.6 — Retain the tendency

**On-screen text**

> The tier is also continuous, showing where the outcome tends within that
> broader condition.

**Visualization**

- Shift `25_PU` from a generic categorical placement to its continuous position
  (`3.098` in the data).
- Do not show the number in the main story.
- Its position sits just inside At-risk, toward the Critical side.
- Use the node’s position alone; avoid a gauge, scorecard, or needle.

**Motion**

- Use one short, subtle positional adjustment.
- The audience should understand “At-risk, with a tendency,” not a new
  calculation.

### Frame 09.7 — Return to all community systems

**On-screen text**

> Zooming back out shows how the same scenario can produce very different
> conditions from one community water system to another.

**Visualization**

- Pull back from `25_PU`.
- Restore all 74 community-water-system nodes on the metro map.
- Encode every node by discrete tier and continuous position/tendency.
- Keep `25_PU` labeled long enough for the audience to relocate it.
- Do not replace the node field with summary counts; the distribution across
  places is the equity story.

**Motion**

- The tier scale folds back into the metro network.
- Other CWS nodes arrive in their network positions.
- The final composition is the metro map with a legible pattern across nodes.

## Visual grammar

### Stable object

The circle never changes meaning:

- On the metro map, it is a station.
- In the analysis, it is the selected outcome node.
- In the 100-year view, each smaller circle is one simulated water-year result
  for that node.
- At the end, the circle returns to the metro map carrying its tier condition
  and continuous tendency.

### Transformation

`25_PU station`

→ `100 simulated water-year marks`

→ `100 delivery/demand pairs`

→ `100 percent-demand-met dots`

→ `one distribution`

→ `one tiered node`

→ `all CWS nodes`

### Avoiding false temporal meaning

- Never use a line chart for the 100 simulated years.
- Never animate from “year 1” to “year 100.”
- Never use calendar-year labels in the audience-facing view.
- Call them `simulated water years` or `hydrologic conditions`.
- Arrange the marks as a field first, then sort them by outcome to form the
  distribution.
- Explain explicitly that the simulation represents variability, not a
  forecast or historical timeline.

### Color

- Use the existing water blue for delivery.
- Use a neutral demand reference.
- Introduce the tier palette only after the distribution has been understood.
- Pair tier color with both the named condition and position.
- Keep non-selected stations quiet until the final zoom-out.

### Layout

- Preserve as much of the metro map as possible through Section 08.
- Let the data view dominate Section 09 while keeping `25_PU` visually
  connected to its station origin.
- Keep copy to one or two short statements per frame.
- Use a consistent text position so the visualization carries the
  transformation.
